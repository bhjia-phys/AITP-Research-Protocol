# Review the exposition of a draft

Use this for a requested review of a paper, a lecture or a substantial note, and on a
finished draft before it is shared. It covers what the reader must be able to do with
the text. Scientific validity is judged separately, with
[manuscript review](../../aitp-verify/SKILL.md#review-a-manuscript) and the checks it
names; a clear text can be wrong and a correct one can be unusable.

Each pass reads the draft for one question. A full or referee-style review runs all of
them; a focused request runs the passes it names. Read what the draft says, and do not
repair a missing step from your own knowledge while reviewing it: the first step you had
to supply is a finding.

## The passes

**Argument.** Make a [reverse outline](../SKILL.md#revise-the-argument-as-a-whole) from
the text as written: what each section and difficult paragraph establishes, what supports
it, and what uses it. Does the chain reach the stated thesis? Is anything established and
never used, or used and never established? Do prerequisites precede their use? Does a
repeated passage add a reason or a needed reminder?

**Claims.** Collect every claim in the title, abstract, introduction, conclusion and
captions, and find where the body establishes each one. Compare the strength of each
statement with its evidence using the [claim table](paper-design.md#state-each-claim-at-the-strength-of-its-evidence):
a condition dropped between body and abstract, a verb strengthened in the conclusion,
numerical agreement described as proof, an interpretation stated as a result, a
conjecture that later sections use as a premise. List claims with no support in the
paper or its cited sources.

**Equations.** For each displayed equation, ask: what is its role (definition, assumption,
approximation, identity used, decisive step, result, check)? Is every symbol defined
before or at the display, with its convention? Does the text say what operation leads to
it from the previous display and why the operation is allowed? Does the text say what the
reader should notice in it? Is its status clear: exact, to which order, under which
assumption, or numerical? Flag "it is easy to see", "after some algebra" and "one finds"
where the step is conceptual rather than mechanical, and displays that nothing uses.

**Notation and consistency.** One symbol for one object, and one name for one concept,
throughout the main text, appendices and figures. Signs, factors of $2\pi$, units and
normalizations agree, or the change is announced. The model, parameters and conventions
in the introduction are the ones the derivation and the figures use. A figure's axes and
caption define their quantities and conditions.

**Transitions.** Read the first sentence of each paragraph after the last sentence of the
previous one. Does it say how this step follows from, qualifies or departs from what came
before? A connective such as "therefore", "however" or "moreover" must match a real
logical relation; paragraphs that only announce what comes next, or restate what was just
shown, are candidates for removal.

**Positioning.** Are the closest prior results cited with their actual claims and
conditions? Does the text say what differs and why it matters? Are "first", "novel" and
"general" supported by the stated comparison? Is credit for imported steps given where
they are used?

**Placement.** Does the main text keep the definitions, the decisive step, the hypothesis
that licenses it and the result? Does an appendix introduce the reason a result holds? Does
supplementary material hide a step the central claim depends on? Is the depth right for
the stated reader: neither unexplained jumps nor explanation of routine algebra?

## Report the findings

For each material finding give:

- **where:** section, paragraph, equation, figure or page;
- **what fails:** the inference that does not follow, the reader need that is not met, or
  the claim that exceeds its evidence;
- **why it matters:** what the reader would conclude wrongly, or could not do;
- **smallest adequate correction:** narrowing a claim, adding the missing step, moving or
  defining something, or a new check;
- **kind and severity:** a scientific error, a missing argument, an exposition problem or
  a preference; blocking, important or minor.

Distinguish what editing can repair from what needs research. Keep a correct subresult
when rejecting a broader interpretation, and keep an objection that remains open as
open. Already effective organization needs no rewrite: say so. State what was not read
or not checked.

## A referee-style report

When the researcher asks for a referee report, write it as a referee would, for the
authors and the editor:

1. **What the paper claims**, in your own words, with the conditions, so the authors can
   see whether the paper was understood.
2. **Assessment of each central claim:** supported, partly supported or not supported by
   the paper, and why.
3. **Major issues:** blocking scientific or argumentative problems, numbered.
4. **Minor issues:** local errors, missing definitions, unclear passages, references.
5. **Questions to the authors** whose answers would change the assessment.

Do not recommend acceptance or rejection unless asked, and keep venue fit, which depends
on the venue's current scope, separate from scientific assessment. A review of another
model's report follows [assessing another model's review](../../aitp-verify/SKILL.md#assess-another-models-review).
Worked examples of weak and repaired passages are in [worked paper edits](worked-paper-edits.md).
